Penny stocks under ₹10 in India: a filing-first research checklist

A share price below ₹10 does not automatically mean a company is cheap. The price of one share says very little without the share count, market capitalization, business economics, cash flow, debt, promoter behavior and dilution history behind it.

Updated August 31, 2026 · Educational research framework only · Not a buy list or investment recommendation.

Core risk: sub-₹10 shares can lose most or all of their value. Low liquidity, weak governance, repeated equity issuance, poor cash conversion and abrupt business changes can matter far more than the headline share price.

1. Start with the business, not the ₹10 filter

A price filter is useful only for discovering a universe. The next question is whether the company has a real operating business that can survive for years. Read the latest annual report, investor disclosures and exchange filings. Understand what the company sells, who pays it, where margins come from and what could permanently damage the business.

Revenue quality

Is revenue recurring, project-based, commodity-linked or dependent on one customer? Large sales growth is less useful when receivables and inventory grow even faster.

Profit quality

Separate operating profit from other income, one-off gains and accounting adjustments. A low P/E can be misleading when the earnings are not repeatable.

Business continuity

Check whether the company has repeatedly changed its main business, gone through restructuring or relies on a new story with little audited history.

2. Check operating cash flow against reported profit

Profit is an accounting measure; cash flow shows whether the business is actually converting activity into cash. One negative year is not automatically fatal, especially for project companies or businesses building inventory. Repeated negative operating cash flow while reported profit stays positive deserves deeper investigation.

  • Compare operating cash flow with profit after tax over several years.
  • Review receivable days, inventory days and working-capital growth.
  • Look for large “other current assets” that need explanation.
  • Check whether fresh borrowings or equity are funding routine operations.

3. Treat dilution as a first-class risk

A company can grow while existing shareholders still receive poor outcomes if the share count expands aggressively. Rights issues, warrants, preferential allotments, employee options, FCCB conversions and other convertible instruments can all increase the number of shares competing for the same future earnings.

Do not stop at current shares outstanding. Read exchange filings for approved but not yet converted warrants, FCCBs and other potential equity. Future dilution can be material before it appears in historical per-share data.

4. Review promoters, pledges and related-party behavior

Promoter ownership by itself is neither good nor bad. The useful questions are whether holdings are stable, whether shares are pledged or encumbered, whether promoters are selling heavily, and whether related-party transactions are understandable.

  • Check promoter holding quarter by quarter.
  • Check pledge or encumbrance disclosures directly on the exchange.
  • Distinguish an inter-se promoter transfer from a sale to the public.
  • Read related-party disclosures and loans or advances involving promoter entities.

5. Read the auditor's wording, not only the numbers

Annual reports and limited-review results can contain important qualifications, emphasis-of-matter paragraphs or uncertainty disclosures. A qualified, adverse or disclaimer opinion can be more important than a cheap valuation multiple. Going-concern language, material control weaknesses or repeated unresolved observations should trigger an immediate review.

6. Measure debt using the business model

Debt must be judged in context. Infrastructure and project businesses can carry more working-capital or project debt than an asset-light software company, but repayment ability still matters. Review interest coverage, maturity profile, credit ratings, security provided and whether debt is rising faster than operating cash generation.

Useful source: credit-rating rationales often explain liquidity, debt servicing, customer concentration, order-book execution and working-capital risks more directly than headline financial portals.

7. Verify order books, contracts and “big wins”

For EPC, infrastructure, renewable-energy and similar companies, an order announcement is not the same as cash in the bank. Check the contract value, execution period, customer, cancellation terms, guarantees and the company's historical ability to convert orders into revenue and cash.

8. Use market capitalization, not share price, for valuation context

Two companies can both trade at ₹8 while one has ten times as many shares outstanding. Compare market capitalization, enterprise value, earnings, book value and cash generation. A ₹5 share can be more expensive than a ₹500 share after adjusting for the ownership represented by each share.

9. Define kill switches before buying

For highly speculative micro-caps, write down what would invalidate the thesis before price volatility tests your discipline. Example review triggers include:

  • new material promoter pledge or encumbrance;
  • major unexplained promoter exit;
  • qualified, adverse or disclaimer audit opinion;
  • going-concern warnings or default;
  • repeated large dilution without proportionate business improvement;
  • persistent negative operating cash flow while profit remains positive;
  • sharp credit-rating downgrade or liquidity stress;
  • a fundamental change in the core business that breaks the original thesis.

A falling share price alone is not evidence that the business has improved or deteriorated. Re-check the thesis rather than averaging down mechanically.

10. Use a source hierarchy

  1. NSE/BSE filings and audited financial statements.
  2. Company investor-relations disclosures.
  3. Credit-rating-agency rationales.
  4. Market-data providers for calculation and cross-checking.
  5. News and aggregators for discovery, followed by primary-source verification.

A 15-minute first-pass screen

Pass 1 · Survival

Real operating business, no obvious default, understandable auditor opinion and manageable near-term liquidity.

Pass 2 · Ownership

Promoter trend, pledge status, dilution pipeline and major related-party items.

Pass 3 · Cash

Operating cash flow, receivables, inventory, debt and funding dependence.

Pass 4 · Valuation

Market cap and enterprise value relative to normalized earnings, assets and cash flow—not merely the share price.

What this page deliberately does not provide

This is not a list of the “best penny stocks under ₹10,” a promise of multibagger returns or a substitute for financial advice. The purpose is to make a risky search query more useful by giving investors a repeatable research process and explicit red flags.
